Why Traditional Health Leaflets Aren’t Enough: Rethinking Community Health Communication

Charles Ogunnowo Olorunfemi
Founder & General Manager, Global Villaje Media

Every year, millions of pounds are spent designing, printing and distributing glossy health leaflets. They sit on reception desks in GP surgeries, community centres and libraries. Yet, when health disparities are measured year after year, the same communities remain disengaged, under-diagnosed and hesitant to seek earlier support.

Why does this happen? The issue is rarely a lack of clinical knowledge; it is a fundamental flaw in communication architecture. Traditional health public communications rely heavily on passive, one-way dissemination that ignores cultural nuance, historical trust deficits and how humans actually process risk.

The Limits of One-Way Dissemination

A printed flyer cannot answer an unspoken fear. It cannot address a cultural stigma, and it cannot validate a personal experience. When someone picks up a leaflet describing high blood pressure or prostate cancer symptoms, they are reading clinical text divorced from the social fabric of their daily life.

For Black African and Caribbean communities in particular, healthcare navigation is influenced by generational experiences, community conversations, faith perspectives and varying levels of trust in statutory institutions. When messages arrive in clinical jargon or sterile corporate branding, they are often perceived as remote and disconnected.

"Health literacy is not simply the ability to read a medical sentence; it is the confidence to understand its relevance, ask questions, and take meaningful action."

Culture, Context and the Missing Middle

Between high-level epidemiological guidance and individual patient choices lies what we call the "missing middle": the trusted communal spaces where beliefs are debated and decisions are made.

Whether in barbershops, places of worship, family dining rooms or community radio broadcasts, health decisions are social. When health institutions bypass these spaces in favour of passive leaflet drops, they miss the opportunity to foster authentic dialogue.

What Culturally Responsive Communication Looks Like

Transforming health communication from passive output into active engagement requires four foundational shifts:

1. Meet people in trusted spaces

Utilise channels that communities already listen to and trust—such as community radio, conversational podcasts, video shorts and grassroots workshops—rather than relying solely on hospital waiting rooms.

2. Combine clinical clarity with human warmth

Pair medical consultants and public health leads with community champions and people who have navigated the condition firsthand. Lived experience provides the bridge that makes clinical facts believable and actionable.

3. Turn monologues into conversations

Structured, accessible broadcast dialogues—like The Health, Wellness & Wellbeing Hour—allow listeners to hear real questions being asked and answered with empathy, demystifying intimidating medical jargon.

4. Create reusable multimedia assets

A single 50-minute health discussion can be distilled into short video clips for social media, audio explainers for messaging apps, and QR-enabled community cards through packages like Campaign in a Box.

From Information to Real Understanding

To close health inequality gaps across the UK, we must move past the assumption that printing more leaflets equals community engagement. By investing in culturally grounded storytelling, broadcast dialogue and accessible media, healthcare organisations can build the enduring trust that saves lives.
